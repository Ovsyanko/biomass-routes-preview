Biogas technologies: feedstocks, equipment, economics and markets

From organic feedstocks to biogas, energy, transport fuel and fertiliser products.

Фото: Florian Gerlach · CC BY-SA 3.0

Biogas is produced when microorganisms break down organic material without oxygen. A biogas plant is not only an energy facility: it is also a controlled waste-treatment system and a source of digestate that can return nutrients to agriculture.

Biogas and the digestion process

Raw biogas normally contains about 50–70% methane, with most of the balance being carbon dioxide. Water vapour, hydrogen sulphide, ammonia and trace compounds determine the cleaning needed for each end use.

Anaerobic digestion proceeds through hydrolysis, acidogenesis, acetogenesis and methanogenesis. Stable operation depends on maintaining a workable pH, temperature, organic loading rate, retention time and ammonia concentration.

Technology classification

Wet digestion is used for pumpable manure, food-industry residues and organic wastewater. Dry or solid-state digestion is suited to stackable municipal biowaste, crop residues and litter manure. Plants may be mesophilic, usually around 35–40°C, or thermophilic, commonly around 50–57°C. The process may be continuous or batch-operated and may use one or several biological stages.

Feedstocks and energy potential

Gas yield depends on total solids, volatile solids, biodegradability and the content of fats, proteins, carbohydrates and lignin. Values per tonne of fresh material are particularly sensitive to water content. Project design should therefore use representative sampling and biochemical methane potential testing.

One normal cubic metre of methane contains about 9.97 kWh. In CHP service, it can typically yield about 3.5–4.2 kWh of electricity and 4–5 kWh of useful heat before plant parasitic consumption.

Main plant systems

A complete installation normally includes feedstock reception and storage, contaminant removal, size reduction and mixing, digestion, gas storage, desulphurisation and drying, CHP or upgrading equipment, digestate storage and process control. Pumps, mixers and gas engines require planned redundancy and maintenance access.

Using biogas and biomethane

Biogas can fuel boilers or combined heat and power units. After carbon dioxide, sulphur compounds and moisture are removed, biomethane can be injected into a gas grid, compressed as CBG or liquefied as LBG for transport. Nordic markets provide established examples of buses, municipal fleets and heavy trucks using biomethane.

One cubic metre of biogas at approximately 60% methane can typically produce 2.0–2.5 kWh of electricity and 2.3–3.0 kWh of useful heat. Overall CHP fuel utilisation may reach 75–90% when the heat has a genuine year-round use.

Digestate management

Digestion does not remove nitrogen, phosphorus or potassium. Digestate may be applied as a liquid fertiliser, separated into liquid and solid fractions, composted, dried, pelletised or processed for nutrient recovery. Its route must be planned before the plant is built. Storage emissions, nutrient balance, pathogens, contaminants and national fertiliser and waste rules all require control.

Economics

Biogas economics combine energy sales, avoided waste-treatment expenditure, gate fees, digestate value and emissions benefits. They must also include feedstock transport, parasitic energy use, engine and upgrading maintenance, digestate handling and grid connection.

IEA estimates span approximately USD 2–20/MBtu for raw biogas, with a European average near USD 16/MBtu. Upgrading may add USD 2–4/MBtu and grid connection about USD 3/MBtu. Heat use usually makes CHP materially stronger than electricity-only operation.

World market

European biogas and biomethane production reached about 22 bcm of natural-gas equivalent in 2024, including 5.2 bcm of biomethane. Installed European biomethane capacity reached approximately 8.2 bcm per year by the end of the second quarter of 2026. The EU’s REPowerEU objective is 35 bcm of annual biomethane production by 2030.

The United States has roughly 2,600 operating biogas systems across farms, wastewater plants, food-waste facilities and landfills. Germany remains Europe’s largest established biogas market, while Sweden and Finland demonstrate the role of upgraded gas in transport. The United Kingdom combines agricultural and municipal digestion with substantial gas-grid injection.

Conclusion

A successful biogas project is defined by the reliability of the whole chain: feedstock, preparation, digestion, gas use and digestate management. Maximum laboratory methane yield or installed engine capacity alone does not determine commercial performance.
