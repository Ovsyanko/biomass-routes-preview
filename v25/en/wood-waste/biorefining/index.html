Biorefining: recover higher-value fractions before energy use

Wood, bark and agricultural residues are not only fuels. They are complex biological raw materials containing extractives, lignin, cellulose, hemicelluloses, minerals and other compounds. A biorefinery seeks to separate and upgrade these fractions before the remaining material is converted to energy, carbon products or other bulk outputs.

The core principle is cascading use: first recover the fraction with the highest practical value, then use the residue in a lower-value but robust process. A bark stream, for example, can yield tannins or polyphenols, after which the extracted solid can still be burned, pelletised or pyrolysed.

Which feedstocks are most interesting?

The best feedstocks combine a concentrated target compound, a large and stable annual flow, low contamination and a useful outlet for the extraction residue. Bark from sawmills and pulp mills, conifer residues, kraft-pulping side streams, lignin-rich streams, clean wood residues and selected agricultural by-products are therefore particularly relevant.

A laboratory result showing that a compound can be extracted is not enough. Industrial attractiveness depends on concentration in the real feedstock, achievable process yield, solvent and heat demand, product purification, market size and the value of the residue.

Tannins: one of the most practical routes

Condensed and hydrolysable tannins are polyphenolic compounds used in leather tanning, adhesives, resins, binders, wood protection and specialty formulations. Bark from selected conifers and hardwoods can contain commercially relevant concentrations.

For forest-industry sites, tannins are attractive because bark is already concentrated at the plant and is often burned at relatively low value. Extracting a soluble fraction before energy recovery can increase value without eliminating the existing energy route.

How much tannin can be recovered from bark?

Yields depend strongly on species, bark fraction, season, storage, particle size and extraction conditions. As an order of magnitude, spruce bark can yield roughly 50–80 kg of tannins per tonne of dry bark, while total dry extract can reach approximately 100–150 kg/t. Pine bark may yield roughly 30–60 kg of tannins per tonne, although actual values vary widely.

These figures should be treated as screening values only. Every project should analyse representative industrial feedstock and confirm recovery in pilot extraction.

How extraction works

Hot water

Hot-water extraction is the simplest industrial entry point. Size-reduced bark is contacted with hot water in batch or continuous equipment, soluble compounds move into the liquid phase, and the solids are separated. The extract is then concentrated and, where required, dried or further purified.

Advantages include low solvent hazard, compatibility with food- or feed-oriented downstream concepts and straightforward integration with steam systems. The disadvantages are large water volumes and the energy required for concentration if evaporation is used.

Why membranes can matter more than the extractor

In many extraction projects, the most expensive operating step is not dissolution of the target compounds but removal of water. Ultrafiltration, nanofiltration and reverse osmosis can remove part of the water and fractionate molecules before thermal evaporation. This can reduce steam consumption and improve product quality.

For this reason, a competitive tannin plant should be designed as an integrated separation system rather than as "an extractor plus a dryer".

Organic solvents

Ethanol and other solvents can improve selectivity for some extractives, but they add solvent recovery, explosion protection, VOC management and fire-safety requirements. They are more suitable for higher-value products whose selling price justifies the added plant complexity.

Supercritical CO₂

Supercritical CO₂ is relevant to lipophilic and high-value specialty compounds. It leaves little solvent residue and can produce clean extracts, but requires high-pressure equipment and is generally a niche route rather than a solution for bulk bark processing.

Conifer chemistry: rosin, turpentine and tall oil

Conifer feedstocks provide a mature example of biorefining. Resin acids, terpenes and fatty acids are converted into rosin, turpentine, tall-oil products and numerous derivatives used in adhesives, inks, rubber, coatings, fragrances and chemical intermediates.

Some routes begin with resinous wood or stumps, while the most industrially developed routes are integrated with kraft pulping, where crude tall oil and turpentine are recovered from process side streams.

Why integration with pulp and paper is especially attractive

A pulp mill already has large biomass flows, steam and recovery systems, chemical handling, laboratories and continuous operations. Side-stream recovery can therefore be implemented with lower marginal infrastructure than at a stand-alone greenfield site.

Tall oil, turpentine and kraft lignin are examples of how a conventional pulp mill can evolve toward a multiproduct biorefinery without abandoning its main cellulose business.

Lignin: huge resource base, much smaller merchant market

Lignin is one of the largest renewable aromatic-carbon resources. Pulping processes separate large quantities of it, but most lignin is still burned for process energy and chemical recovery.

Commercial lignin products include lignosulfonates, kraft lignin, dispersants, concrete admixtures, binders and specialty formulations. Higher-purity lignin is also being developed as a partial phenol substitute, a polyurethane component, a precursor for carbon materials and a feedstock for aromatic chemicals.

The central challenge is not raw-material availability but market depth. The potential lignin resource is far larger than the current merchant market, so a project cannot assume that all recovered lignin will find a high-value buyer.

Sugars and fermentation chemistry

Cellulose and hemicelluloses can be converted to fermentable C5 and C6 sugars through pretreatment and hydrolysis. In theory, dry lignocellulosic biomass can contain roughly 400–600 kg of potentially fermentable sugars per tonne, although practical recovery is lower.

The sugars can feed production of ethanol, lactic acid, succinic acid and other biochemicals. The challenge is CAPEX, enzyme and pretreatment cost, inhibitor formation, fermentation yield and the need for large-scale downstream markets.

Biorefining plus carbonisation

One particularly attractive cascade for bark and clean wood residues is:

extraction → dewatering/drying → pyrolysis → charcoal, biochar or industrial biocarbon.

Extraction removes only a fraction of the mass. The remaining lignocellulosic solid still contains most of the structural carbon and can be converted thermochemically.

Energy integration with pyrolysis

Pyrolysis gas can provide heat for extraction, concentration and drying. Conversely, extraction can remove compounds that would otherwise enter pyrolysis condensates. A properly integrated site can therefore create both a chemical product and a carbon product while reducing purchased heat demand.

Another route: extraction plus pellet production

The extracted residue can also be dried and pelletised. The main economic risk is water: wet extraction creates a residue that may require substantial re-drying. Unless low-cost heat is available, energy consumption can consume much of the value added by extraction.

Three practical entry routes for the forest industry

For an international forest-industry project, three routes are especially practical to screen first:

  1. bark → technical tannin or polyphenol extract;
  2. conifer side streams → resin, rosin, turpentine and tall-oil products;
  3. lignin → binders, dispersants and material applications.

These routes are technologically more mature than a fully integrated greenfield lignocellulosic biorefinery producing multiple specialty chemicals.

1. Bark → technical tannins

A commercial concept can use hot-water extraction, solid-liquid separation, membrane concentration and final evaporation or drying. The residue remains available for energy or carbon products.

2. Conifer resin products

Rosin, turpentine, tall oil and their derivatives are part of an established industrial sector. The global pine-derived chemicals market has been estimated at roughly $6.4 billion in 2025. This is a mature route where value creation comes from integration, fractionation and derivatives rather than from proving the basic chemistry.

3. Lignin → binders and materials

Lignosulfonates already have large-scale commercial uses as dispersants, binders and concrete admixtures. Kraft lignin is increasingly available as a merchant product, while higher-value applications such as partial phenol replacement, polyurethane chemistry and carbon materials require more purification and customer-specific development.

Where is the most attractive zone?

The strongest projects often lie between two extremes. At one extreme, simply burning bark leaves chemical value unextracted. At the other, a fully integrated multi-product biorefinery can require hundreds of millions of euros and expose the investor to several new markets at once.

A practical middle ground is a modular extraction plant integrated with an existing forest-industry site, using existing utilities and retaining a proven outlet for the residue.

What makes a project investable?

  • a large, stable and traceable feedstock stream;
  • laboratory confirmation of extract content in the actual industrial material;
  • a buyer or qualification programme before scale-up;
  • low-cost steam or recoverable process heat;
  • membrane or other low-energy concentration where appropriate;
  • a profitable outlet for the extracted residue;
  • a staged path from pilot to commercial plant.

Conclusion

Biorefining changes the question from "how can this biomass be disposed of or burned?" to "which valuable fractions should be recovered before the residue enters an energy or materials route?"

For existing forest-industry sites, the most practical opportunities often lie in modular integration: bark extraction, conifer side-stream recovery, lignin products and cascades that retain a reliable outlet for the residual biomass.

The investment case depends less on theoretical chemistry than on three practical factors: real feedstock composition, real achievable selling volume and the cost of separating water. Where those three are favourable, biorefining can materially increase value created per tonne of biomass without sacrificing the energy value of the residue.
