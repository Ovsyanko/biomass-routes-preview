Wood-composite comparison

Arbolit, wood wool, CBPB, MDF, particleboard and Vihrolyt

The same wood feedstock can lead to very different materials. A comparison of structure, process, strengths and constraints.

Illustration: engineered wood selection, Raysonho · CC0

Wood-based materials differ in more than name. Filler geometry, binder, density and forming method determine whether the result is a light block, an acoustic board, a dense exterior sheet or a smooth furniture panel.

Production approaches

Arbolit

Coarse filler creates a porous bulk material; chip quality, matrix compatibility and forming consistency are critical.

Wood wool board

Long strands create open texture and acoustic activity; the process is primarily board-oriented.

CBPB

Finer filler in a dense cement matrix produces a heavy, stiff sheet for mechanical and weather exposure.

MDF and particleboard

Hot pressing of organic-bonded fibres or particles gives scale and smoothness but a different fire, moisture and emission profile.

Vihrolyt

The concept centres on a controlled plant–mineral structure across a density range. Formulations and critical settings remain know-how.

How to choose

There is no universally best technology. The decision starts with the pair “available feedstock – product the regional market can absorb”, not with a machine. The same mass of wood yields different volumes of finished material because mineral content, density, thickness and preparation losses differ.

Feedstock morphology is part of the process. Being able to use a residue does not mean feeding it without cleaning, drying, size reduction or fractionation.

Scale and capital requirement

The common basis is 5,000 tonnes of oven-dry wood per year. Land, VAT, working capital and off-site infrastructure are excluded. This stream is far below a rational stand-alone MDF or particleboard mill, so their rows show the order of magnitude of a minimum industrial plant that would need a much larger fibre basket.

Cost per square metre is an arithmetic conversion at the stated thickness, not a comparison of functionally equivalent assemblies. A 300 mm wall block is not directly comparable with a 12–16 mm lining sheet.

  1. Market first: define the product, substitution price, buyer requirements and delivery radius.
  2. Then morphology: run a sieve analysis and assess moisture, bark, mineral contamination, seasonality and stabilisation options.
  3. Then scale: match guaranteed annual supply to minimum line utilisation. Free wood cannot rescue an under-loaded MDF or particleboard mill.
  4. Validate the product: make samples and test strength, thermal conductivity, fire, water, emissions, fasteners and durability.
  5. Budget last: obtain equipment proposals and calculate the full mass, energy, labour, reject, logistics and working-capital balance.
