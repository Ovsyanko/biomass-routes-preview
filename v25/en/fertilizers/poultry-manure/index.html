Fertilisers from poultry manure and litter

Processing routes, products, equipment and market.

Photo: сельскохозяйственное поле · CC BY 2.0

Poultry farms produce not only meat and eggs but also substantial quantities of manure and litter: excreta mixed, depending on the housing system, with straw, sawdust, sunflower husk and other bedding materials.

If poorly managed, these streams become sources of odour, ammonia emissions and contamination of soil and surface water. If properly processed, they become feedstock for compost, growing media, dry organic fertilisers and organo-mineral fertilisers.

The project objective is not simply to dry or remove the waste. It is to create a safe, stable and usable commercial product with controlled properties.

The poultry-manure management problem

The amount and properties of manure or manure-litter mix depend on bird type and age, housing technology, bedding type and dosage, feed formulation, drinker management and cleaning frequency.

Fresh poultry excreta often have very high moisture, while broiler litter can be substantially drier because bedding absorbs water. The nutrient value cannot be taken from a single reference table: it should be confirmed by analysis of the actual feedstock.

Typical challenges include:

  • high and variable moisture;
  • heterogeneous composition;
  • ammonia emissions and strong odour;
  • possible pathogens, parasite eggs and viable weed seeds;
  • leachate generation during storage;
  • nitrogen loss;
  • caking and poor flowability;
  • high transport cost for material containing a large share of water;
  • seasonal land-application demand while the material is generated continuously;
  • the need for dedicated pads, equipment, monitoring and documentation.

Regulatory classification differs by country. In an international project, the manure or litter must be handled according to the local rules for animal by-products, fertilisers, emissions, water protection and waste or by-product status. The technical design should therefore be separated from country-specific permitting analysis.

Processing can solve environmental, operational and commercial problems at the same time.

Products that can be manufactured

Compost

Compost is produced by aerobic biological stabilisation of manure or litter. It can be used in agriculture, growing-media production, landscaping and land reclamation.

Loose compost generally requires less equipment than drying and granulation, but its lower bulk density and remaining moisture usually limit the economical transport radius.

Dry powdered fertiliser

Drying and milling produce a homogeneous dry material that can be sold directly, used in growing-media manufacture or sent to granulation. The main disadvantage of powder is dust; granules are usually easier to transport, meter and spread mechanically.

Granulated organic fertiliser

Granules are made by drying, milling and compacting the organic material. Commercial products are commonly cylindrical pellets or granules in the 4–6 mm range. Diameters around 4–5 mm are compatible with many fertiliser spreaders and seed drills; 6 mm pellets can be easier and cheaper to manufacture and often have good mechanical strength.

Organo-mineral fertiliser

Mineral nutrients, trace elements, liming materials or functional additives can be incorporated into the organic base to create a controlled formulation. This allows producers to adjust N:P:K ratios, create recipes for different crop groups, add calcium, magnesium, sulphur or micronutrients and improve batch consistency.

Mineral components may be introduced before granulation or coated onto finished granules. The formulation should be based on chemical analysis of the organic base and agronomic requirements of the target crop.

Growing media and soil blends

Compost or dry fertiliser can be blended with peat alternatives, sand, plant fibres, mineral fillers and structure-forming components. Customers include greenhouses, nurseries, landscaping companies, municipalities and the garden-retail market.

Typical characteristics of granulated fertiliser

Poultry manure contains nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and trace elements, but the composition varies materially with diet, bird type, bedding, storage and processing conditions. Advantages of a granulated product

Lower logistics and storage cost

Drying removes water and granulation increases bulk density. The product requires less storage space per tonne of nutrients, does not generate the same leachate problems as fresh manure and can be transported much farther economically.

Mechanised application

Granules can be metered and spread with conventional equipment more consistently than wet manure or irregular litter.

Higher marketability

A stable dry product can be screened, packaged, branded and sold through wholesale, professional horticulture and retail channels. This opens markets that are inaccessible to fresh manure.

Retention of organic matter

Unlike complete combustion, composting, drying and granulation retain organic carbon and a large share of the nutrient value in a soil-oriented product.

Reduced environmental burden at the farm

Correct processing reduces the volume of unstable raw material held on site, lowers odour and leachate risk and creates a planned outlet for a continuously generated stream.

Limitations

The route also has important constraints. Drying high-moisture manure can require large amounts of heat; ammonia and odour emissions must be controlled; gas cleaning can be necessary; granulation adds electricity use and wear; and final product quality is constrained by the chemistry of the original manure.

Heavy metals, salts, pathogens and contaminants must remain within applicable fertiliser rules. Processing cannot compensate for poor feedstock control.

Processing technology

Composting as an option

Composting can be a stand-alone route or a pretreatment step. Aerobic microbial activity stabilises readily degradable organic matter, generates heat and can contribute to hygienisation. It also requires time, aeration, working area and moisture control, and can cause nitrogen losses if ammonia is not managed.

Composting is not mandatory before drying and granulation. Where the raw litter has appropriate moisture and composition, a direct drying–milling route can be technically preferable.

Drying and granulation

A typical industrial route includes receiving and storage, removal of foreign objects, metering and mixing, drying, milling or simultaneous drying-milling, gas cleaning, intermediate storage, granulation, cooling, screening, return of fines and packaging.

Feedstock preparation

Stable flow into the dryer is essential. Long fibres, stones, metal and oversized objects should be removed. Very wet manure may require mixing with drier litter or recycled dry product to improve handling and reduce sticking.

AS-4 drying-milling unit

One process configuration considered in the underlying project uses an AS-4 type drying-milling unit. Its purpose is to combine moisture removal and particle-size preparation in a single apparatus before pelletising. The applicability of this equipment depends on the actual feedstock's moisture, stickiness, fibre content, required throughput and available heat source, and should be confirmed in pilot testing.

Granulation

The prepared dry material is conditioned as required and fed to a pellet mill or granulator. After forming, granules are cooled, screened and sent to storage or packaging. Fines can normally be returned to the process.

Production economics

Project economics are driven by four main groups of factors.

First, the value of solving the manure-management problem. The poultry producer can reduce the cost of storage, handling, treatment and removal while lowering environmental and regulatory risk.

Second, feedstock preparation cost. When moisture is high, thermal energy is usually the dominant variable. Every extra tonne of evaporated water increases fuel consumption, heat-generator duty and required dryer capacity.

Third, granulation and product finishing. Electricity, die and roller wear, cooling, screening, packaging and storage all add cost.

Fourth, selling price and sales stability. A project should not be based on the highest retail price. Large volumes are normally sold into professional channels at wholesale or contract prices.

A rational product portfolio can combine bulk 4–6 mm granules in big bags, dry powder for growing-media manufacturers, small packs for garden retail, higher-value organo-mineral formulations, local loose compost and private-label contract production.

Conclusions

Processing poultry manure and litter into fertiliser is one of the most direct ways to return organic matter and nutrients to soil while solving an environmental management problem at the poultry farm.

The main advantages are a large continuously renewable feedstock resource, lower environmental burden, creation of a standardised commercial product, compatibility with mechanised application, reduced storage and transport cost, access to several customer segments and the ability to produce both organic and organo-mineral formulations.

Composting can be either a final route or a pretreatment stage, but it is not mandatory. Litter with suitable properties can be dried and milled directly before granulation.

Where drying-milling equipment such as an AS-4 is considered, the result is determined by the whole process system — receiving, heat generation, drying, gas cleaning, cooling, granulation, packaging and sales — not by a single machine. Pilot testing on the actual manure or litter remains essential before industrial design.
