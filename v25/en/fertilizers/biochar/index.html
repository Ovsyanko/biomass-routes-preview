Biochar in agriculture and urban greening

Soil effects, research, markets, carbon removals and project economics.

Биочар

What is biochar?

Biochar is a porous, carbon-rich material produced by pyrolysis of wood, agricultural residues and other biomass under limited oxygen. Technologically it is close to charcoal, but the term biochar is defined above all by the intended use of the carbon: instead of being burned, the carbon is retained and the material is used in soils, growing media, compost, green infrastructure and other long-lived applications.

This distinction matters. The same basic pyrolysis platform can potentially produce fuel charcoal, industrial biocarbon, activated-carbon precursor or biochar for agriculture and landscaping, but feedstock requirements, contaminant limits, pyrolysis temperature, ash content, particle-size distribution and traceability requirements differ between these products.

USDA describes biochar as a stable carbon-rich material made from biomass that has been partially thermally decomposed under limited oxygen, and emphasises that its properties depend strongly on both feedstock and production temperature.

The practical conclusion is important: biochar is not one standardised material. Clean-wood biochar produced at 600–700°C, straw char, manure-derived char and char produced from sewage sludge can behave very differently in soil.

How can biochar improve soil?

The main effects are associated with its porous structure and relatively persistent carbon matrix. Depending on feedstock, production conditions, soil and dose, a suitable biochar can:

  • increase soil water-holding capacity, particularly in light, sandy or degraded soils;
  • retain nutrients, reducing leaching and creating a longer-term sorption reservoir;
  • increase cation-exchange capacity as particle surfaces age and oxidise;
  • change pH. Many wood biochars are alkaline and can be useful on acidic soils, while excessive application to alkaline soils can be undesirable;
  • provide microporous habitat for soil microorganisms;
  • improve physical structure and aeration in compacted soils;
  • reduce the mobility of some contaminants and heavy metals through sorption;
  • increase the stock of persistent organic carbon in soil.

Biochar should therefore be viewed not as a conventional NPK fertiliser but as a soil amendment, nutrient carrier and substrate component.

There is an important caveat: fresh dry char can initially adsorb dissolved nutrients from soil. Many practical systems therefore "charge" it before application by mixing it with compost, manure, digestate, liquid organic fertiliser or another nutrient solution.

What do studies show?

The scientific literature is now large enough to distinguish recurring trends from isolated spectacular results.

A global analysis of field experiments published in Agricultural Water Management in 2024 reported average increases of approximately 11.2% in crop yield and 14.8% in water-use productivity. Effects were strongest on low-fertility and low-pH soils, and wood-derived biochar performed well in the dataset.

Another meta-analysis covering 381 datasets from 63 studies reported:

  • 14.45% higher crop yield;
  • 14.28% higher water-use efficiency;
  • 13.97% higher nitrogen-use efficiency.

A still broader synthesis of 592 publications and almost 10,000 observations found, in field trials, roughly 26.6% higher soil organic-carbon stocks, 15.7% higher crop yield, about 14.8% lower CH₄ emissions, 23.1% lower N₂O emissions and 23.2% less inorganic-nitrogen leaching.

These averages do not mean that every farm will obtain a 10–15% yield increase. Results vary widely with soil, climate, crop, dose, feedstock, production temperature and whether the biochar is used alone or in a blended fertiliser.

Long-term effects

Long-term evidence matters because the business case often assumes benefits lasting more than one season. A 2024 global assessment based on 981 observation series found an average long-term crop-yield increase of around 16%; even after six years the effect remained close to 15%. In trials lasting more than two years, soil organic carbon was on average about 36.2% higher.

A six-year rice-field study in China in which 20 and 40 t/ha of rice-straw biochar were applied once reported total-phosphorus leaching reductions of 6.77–17.62%, dissolved reactive phosphorus reductions of 6.22–10.28% and increases in rice phosphorus-use efficiency of 5.33–10.33%. The authors also estimated an additional economic benefit of 2,558–4,655 yuan/ha per year from higher yields.

There are also neutral or cautious findings. A 2026 review by the Swiss Federal Office for the Environment, for example, did not consider yield benefits sufficiently established to recommend indiscriminate large-scale application in Swiss agriculture and highlighted product cost, competition for biomass and the need to understand long-term impacts on soil biota. This is an important counterweight to marketing claims that biochar is a universal soil improver.

Where is application most justified?

The highest-value applications are not necessarily on already fertile arable land. Benefits are often more pronounced on sandy soils with poor water retention, acidic soils, degraded low-humus land, drought-prone sites, high-value vegetable and horticultural systems, and where biochar is blended with compost or fertiliser.

USDA also highlights degraded or problematic soils, forest and rangeland restoration and reclamation. Where carbon removal is monetised as well as the physical product, applications that would otherwise be too expensive can become commercially viable.

Main biochar markets

1. Agriculture

Agriculture is the most obvious potential market and at the same time one of the most difficult economically. Typical applications include arable crops, vegetables, orchards, vineyards, greenhouses, nurseries, growing-media blends and restoration of degraded land.

Research doses often range from 5 to 20 t/ha, while experimental studies also use 20–40 t/ha or more. Such doses can be too costly for commodity farming. The market is therefore moving toward more formulated products such as biochar + compost, biochar + mineral fertiliser, biochar + organic fertiliser, microbial carriers, and granulated or pelletised soil improvers.

For many projects, the more promising business model is not selling "black powder" but selling a functional soil or fertiliser product in which biochar is one component.

2. Composting and organic-waste processing

Biochar can be added to compost, manure, poultry litter, organic sludges and residues, or biogas digestate. It can bind moisture and ammonium, reduce odour and provide a more persistent carbon matrix.

This is particularly interesting for a biochar producer because the material can first perform a process function as a sorbent and then be applied to soil together with the organic matter.

3. Biogas

Biochar is used experimentally and commercially as an additive to anaerobic digesters and as a treatment component for digestate. Proposed mechanisms include microbial immobilisation on the porous surface, improved direct interspecies electron transfer, reduced inhibition by ammonia or some contaminants, and improved process stability.

The same biochar can then leave the plant in nutrient-rich digestate and be used as a soil amendment. This dual use — process additive first, nutrient carrier and soil amendment second — is one of the route's most interesting economic features.

4. Urban greening, municipalities and parks

This segment can be more attractive than its physical volume suggests. A municipality can combine management of woody and green residues, useful heat from pyrolysis, production of an urban-soil component, improved drought resilience of plantings and local long-term carbon removal.

Helsinki provides a useful example. In 2022–2023 the city, together with Aalto University and other partners, tested biochar production from urban organic residues and created 10 demonstration sites in parks, tree plantings, lawns, tram corridors and sports areas. Wood-chip biochar contained roughly 82% carbon, and about 75 m³ of biochar was produced during the pilot. Some green-waste feedstocks were rejected because of elevated metals, illustrating why feedstock control is essential.

5. Reclamation and remediation

Biochar's sorption capacity, alkalinity in many grades and persistent carbon structure make it relevant to mine-site restoration, degraded soils, contaminated land and other reclamation applications. In such projects the value is determined not only by crop yield but by the cost of restoring land to a required ecological or engineering condition.

Biochar and the carbon-removal market

When biomass grows, it removes CO₂ from the atmosphere. If the biomass is then burned or decomposes, much of that carbon quickly returns to the atmosphere. Pyrolysis can convert part of the biogenic carbon into a much more stable aromatic matrix.

If the resulting biochar is placed in a durable application where it is not burned or rapidly oxidised, this creates Biochar Carbon Removal (BCR).

How much CO₂ can one tonne of biochar store?

A simple physical calculation illustrates the scale. If one tonne of dry biochar contains 80% carbon:

0.8 t C × 44/12 = 2.93 t CO₂ equivalent.

Not all of that carbon is accepted as durable, and emissions from feedstock collection, transport, drying, production, electricity and product logistics have to be deducted. The number of certified removal credits is therefore lower.

In one verified Puro.earth project, for example, the confirmed factor was 2.241 CORC per tonne of biochar, or roughly 2.24 t of net removed CO₂ per tonne of product. Each project must calculate its own factor.

A carbon credit is not an automatic premium for producing charcoal

High-quality BCR credits require evidence of sustainable biomass origin, pyrolysis conditions, carbon content and stability, lifecycle emissions, avoidance of double counting, durable end use and traceability of each batch.

Under the 2025 Puro.earth methodology, qualifying biochar is treated as CORC200+, indicating storage durability measured in centuries. Measurement, reporting and verification — MRV — is what converts a physical tonne of biochar into a certifiable climate product.

Size of the BCR market

According to CDR.fyi, from the beginning of 2022 to mid-2025 roughly 3.04 million tonnes of CO₂ of biochar removals had been contracted, including around 1.6 million tonnes in the first six months of 2025 alone. By mid-2025 approximately 683,000 tonnes of BCR had been delivered and about 330,000 tonnes retired.

Estimated transaction value grew from about $14.6 million in 2022 to $33.9 million in 2023 and $181.5 million in 2024, equivalent to a calculated CAGR of roughly 132% over that period.

By the first quarter of 2026, durable CDR contracts totalled about 2.3 million tonnes for the quarter, with approximately 93% attributed to biochar carbon removal. The market is still highly dependent on large corporate buyers. Microsoft, Google, BCG, JPMorgan and others have signed multi-year removal contracts; one Microsoft–Exomad Green agreement announced in 2025 covered roughly 1.24 million tonnes of CO₂.

Carbon-removal pricing

BCR prices are much higher than low-cost conventional offset credits. In a CDR.fyi supplier survey for 2025, the average estimated break-even price was about $143/t CO₂, while the price associated with a reasonable profit was around $187/t CO₂. For comparison, the volume-weighted market price for biochar removals in 2023 was estimated at roughly $131/t CO₂.

If one tonne of product generates, for example, 2.2 tonnes of certified CO₂ removal, a carbon value of $140/t CO₂ corresponds to approximately $310 per tonne of biochar; at $180/t CO₂ it approaches $400/t biochar. This is why carbon finance can fundamentally change project economics.

Physical market scale

Physical production remains small compared with the theoretical application potential. International Biochar Initiative reporting indicated that global biochar production in 2023 exceeded roughly 350,000 t/year, with sector revenue estimated at around $600 million.

European Biochar Industry estimates put installed European capacity at approximately 75,000 t/year in 2023, with an expected 2024 level of around 115,000 t/year. In 2023 Europe added 48 new plants, bringing the number of operating production facilities to about 171.

For scale, global arable and permanent-crop area is around 1.6 billion hectares. A hypothetical one-time application of just 1 t/ha would require 1.6 billion tonnes of biochar, and 5 t/ha would require 8 billion tonnes — orders of magnitude above current output. Near-term market constraints are therefore less about theoretical land area than about the supply of consistent, affordable product and the economics of transport and application.

Which markets are likely to develop first?

Applying tens of tonnes of expensive biochar per hectare of wheat or barley is difficult to justify from yield gains alone in most regions. Commercialisation is therefore advancing first where there is additional value:

  • carbon-removal credits plus sale of physical biochar;
  • horticulture, vegetables, greenhouses and nurseries, where crop value per hectare is high;
  • composts and blended fertilisers, where a smaller dose of a high-value component can improve a formulated product;
  • urban greening, trees and parks, where clients value climate and water-management benefits together;
  • sports turf and specialised growing media;
  • reclamation and remediation, where value is linked to restoration performance;
  • biogas and organic-waste processing, where biochar performs more than one function before entering soil.

Commodity field agriculture could ultimately become the largest physical market, but wider adoption is likely to depend on lower product cost, carbon finance and a shift from very high application rates toward optimised blends and fertiliser formulations.

Limitations and risks

Biochar should not be presented as a universal solution. Important limitations include:

  • Cost. Transporting and spreading several tonnes per hectare can cost more than the agronomic benefit.
  • Highly variable product properties. The word "biochar" alone says very little about quality.
  • Contamination. Unsuitable feedstock or poor pyrolysis control can result in excessive heavy metals, PAHs or other unwanted compounds.
  • pH. Alkaline biochar is not appropriate for every soil.
  • Dust. Fine dry material is difficult to handle; wetting, granulation or blending may be necessary.
  • Temporary nutrient immobilisation. Fresh uncharged biochar can adsorb plant-available nutrients under some conditions.
  • Logistics. Wood biochar has relatively low bulk density.
  • Carbon-credit value is not guaranteed. Credits are created only after methodology requirements and independent verification are met.

Economic model

The most attractive biochar projects do not rely on a single revenue stream. Potential revenues include physical biochar, useful heat, sometimes power or syngas use, gate fees or avoided biomass-disposal costs, carbon-removal credits, and formulated soil or fertiliser products.

This makes modern biochar economics very different from conventional charcoal production. A project that simply sells char to a farmer can be difficult. A project that accepts a low-cost residue, produces useful heat, sells biochar into a higher-value segment and additionally certifies 2–2.5 t of carbon removal per tonne of product can have a fundamentally different financial profile.

Conclusion

Biochar is evolving from a niche agronomic practice into an industry at the intersection of biomass pyrolysis, agriculture, organic fertilisers, urban infrastructure and carbon removal.

Scientific evidence indicates that, where product and soil are appropriately matched, average crop-yield improvements often fall in the range of roughly 10–16%, while water and nutrient efficiency can improve, nutrient losses can decline and soil carbon stocks can increase.

However, the current industrial growth driver is not agriculture alone. Biochar can convert low-value biomass into a useful material, recover energy and lock away part of atmospheric carbon for centuries. The ability of the same tonne of product to function both as a material and as a physical carbon store is what differentiates the emerging biochar market from many other biomass-utilisation routes.
