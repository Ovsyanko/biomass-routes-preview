Bioconversion: feed and mushroom substrates

Feedstocks, production, sales and routes for spent substrate.

Illustration раздела «Агроотходы»

Bioconversion is the use of microorganisms, fungi and enzymatic processes to transform organic feedstocks into products with higher added value.

For some co-products from agriculture, food processing and wood processing, this route can be substantially more valuable than direct combustion or conversion into fuel. Straw, bran, oilseed cakes and meals, grain-processing residues, fruit pomace, brewers' spent grain, selected clean sawdust streams and other plant materials can serve as feedstock for mushroom cultivation, feed ingredients and a wide range of fermentation processes.

The application range is nevertheless narrower than for energy conversion of biomass. When the end product enters the food or feed chain, feedstock origin, composition stability, absence of chemical and microbiological contamination, and full traceability become critical.

The most valuable bioconversion feedstocks are therefore usually relatively clean, homogeneous co-products generated directly at a controlled production site, rather than mixed wastes of uncertain origin.

Which biomass streams are suitable for bioconversion?

The potential feedstock base is broad. In agriculture and food processing it includes, among other streams:

  • cereal straw;

  • maize stalks and cobs;

  • husks and shells from agricultural crops;

  • bran;

  • off-spec grain and grain-processing residues;

  • oilseed cakes and meals;

  • sugar-beet pulp;

  • fruit and berry pomace;

  • brewers' spent grain;

  • molasses and other sugar-rich co-products.

For some mushroom technologies, wood-processing feedstocks can include clean sawdust, small wood chips, shavings and other untreated wood particles. Wood must be free from coatings, preservatives, adhesives and other chemical contaminants.

Different fungi and microorganisms have very different feedstock requirements. There is therefore no universal "bioconversion substrate": formulation and preparation have to be designed for the specific biological process.

Mushroom substrates

One of the clearest examples of plant-biomass bioconversion is edible-mushroom cultivation. Low-value lignocellulosic material is converted into a food product with much higher value per unit of mass.

Industrial mushroom farming uses different substrate systems. Button mushrooms are typically grown on composted mixtures based on straw and other organic components. Oyster mushrooms and a number of other species can use straw, sawdust, husks, maize residues and mixtures of plant materials.

The feedstock is size-reduced, moisture and formulation are adjusted, and the material is then pasteurised, sterilised or otherwise treated to suppress competing microorganisms. The prepared substrate is inoculated with mushroom mycelium.

During growth, fungal enzymes partially degrade lignin, cellulose and hemicelluloses. Part of the organic matter is incorporated into the mushroom crop, while a large share remains after the production cycles as spent mushroom substrate (SMS). Managing this material is an important part of the economics and environmental performance of a modern mushroom operation.

Feed products

A second major route is biological or fermentation processing of plant co-products into feed ingredients. Some food-processing residues can be used in feed after relatively simple stabilisation or drying. In other cases, fermentation can materially change their properties.

Technologies include yeast fermentation, lactic-acid fermentation, filamentous-fungi fermentation, solid-state fermentation (SSF) and enzymatic treatment.

Microorganisms consume part of the carbohydrate fraction while creating their own cellular biomass. Depending on the process, this can increase the relative protein content, improve digestibility and reduce some anti-nutritional compounds.

Research on solid-state fermentation demonstrates that a wide range of agro-industrial co-products can be upgraded into more nutritious feed materials. A more advanced route is microbial protein production using yeasts, bacteria or fungi that convert available carbon compounds into protein-rich biomass. This area is developing rapidly, but it requires substantially tighter process control, safety assurance and downstream purification than conventional feed preparation.

Feedstock preparation

The exact line configuration depends on the product, but most processes include several recurring operations.

Receiving and quality control

Feedstock provenance and the absence of undesirable contaminants are especially important. For a food or feed application, chemical composition alone is not enough. Control may need to cover pesticides and other chemical contaminants, heavy metals, mycotoxins, pathogenic microorganisms, foreign material, and storage and transport conditions.

Size reduction

Particle size is adjusted to provide suitable mixing, wetting, heat treatment and biological access.

Formulation adjustment

Nitrogen sources, minerals and supplementary carbon-rich materials may be added. For mushroom substrates, the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, pore structure, air permeability and water-holding capacity are particularly important.

Moisture adjustment

Most biological processes operate at relatively high moisture. This is both an advantage and a challenge: wet feedstock can often be processed without energy-intensive predrying, but the final product or spent substrate can also remain wet and costly to transport.

Pasteurisation or sterilisation

The required treatment intensity depends on the process. The objective is to give the desired fungal or microbial culture a competitive advantage over unwanted organisms.

Biological conversion

The prepared material is inoculated with the selected culture. Residence time can range from hours or days for some fermentation processes to several weeks in mushroom production. Typical control parameters include temperature, moisture, airflow, oxygen and CO₂ concentration and, in some processes, pH and other process indicators.

The core principle: extract the higher-value product first

Bioconversion should be assessed within the broader concept of cascading biomass use. Directly converting a clean food- or agriculture-industry co-product into heat usually creates a relatively low-value output.

If the same tonne can first produce mushrooms, a feed ingredient or another biological product, and only then the remaining organic matter is used for fertiliser or energy, the total value generated from the feedstock can be much higher.

A typical cascade can be represented as:

plant co-product → mushroom or fermentation substrate → primary product → spent substrate → fertiliser / biogas / solid biofuel.

Energy recovery is not excluded. It is moved to the last stage of the cascade, after higher-value products have been extracted.

What can be done with spent mushroom substrate?

Mushroom production leaves a substantial amount of moist organic material. Fungal enzymes have already modified it, but it still contains a large organic fraction. Reviews commonly report organic-matter contents of roughly 40–60% in SMS, depending on the original substrate and cultivation process. Treating it simply as waste therefore discards potential value.

Reuse as substrate

In some systems, a share of SMS can be reused in a new cultivation cycle, either directly or after blending with fresh material. The number of reuse cycles is limited by changes in structure and nutrient balance, mineral accumulation and sanitary requirements.

Compost and organic fertiliser

This is one of the most natural routes. Because fungal growth has already partially decomposed the plant biomass, SMS can be a suitable input to further composting. After stabilisation it can be used as an organic fertiliser, soil improver or component of growing media.

Commercial products require control of nutrient content, soluble salts, pH, residual organic matter, sanitary indicators and batch-to-batch consistency. A loose local compost and a screened, standardised and packaged soil product are economically very different products.

Biogas production

SMS can be directed to anaerobic digestion. This is attractive because fungal pretreatment has already opened part of the lignocellulosic structure, potentially making some organic fractions more accessible to the anaerobic microbial community.

The process yields biogas for heat, power or upgrading to biomethane and a digestate containing nutrients and residual organic matter. Co-digestion with manure, food-processing residues or other wet organics can be more practical than building a dedicated small digester around SMS alone.

The decision should be based on biochemical methane potential, moisture, fibre content, transport distance and the availability of a suitable digestate outlet.

Fuel pellets and other solid fuels

SMS can technically be dried, size-reduced and pelletised. The main difficulty is its high moisture: a large quantity of water may have to be evaporated before pellet production. For this reason, the economics are driven primarily by the availability of low-cost heat.

The route becomes more attractive where there is boiler waste heat, CHP heat, inexpensive biomass fuel, solar-assisted drying or another low-grade heat source. If expensive purchased fuel is required solely to remove water, composting or anaerobic digestion can be more competitive.

Ash and mineral content can also be higher than in premium wood pellets. Such pellets should therefore usually be positioned as agricultural or industrial solid fuel, not as a direct substitute for premium residential wood pellets.

Other conversion routes

More advanced routes for SMS and similar biologically pretreated residues are being researched, including bioethanol, hydrogen, pyrolysis and bio-oil, biochar, enzyme recovery, polysaccharides and other bioactive compounds, as well as sorption and bioremediation materials.

Technical potential does not automatically mean commercial readiness. For an investment project it is essential to distinguish a laboratory-proven conversion pathway from a technology with established equipment, industrial references and a real market.

Sales channels

A key difference between bioconversion and commodity fuel production is the much greater specialisation of the markets.

Mushrooms

Typical channels include retail chains, produce wholesalers, markets, food-service operators and food processors. The main value is created by the final food product rather than by the substrate itself.

Prepared mushroom substrates

Potential customers include industrial mushroom farms, smaller growers and operators that buy already inoculated blocks. A business can specialise in substrate production without growing mushrooms itself.

Feed ingredients

Buyers can include feed mills, large livestock farms, poultry producers, pig farms, feed-additive producers and aquaculture companies. Stable composition, demonstrated nutritional value and product safety are critical.

Fertilisers and growing-media products

Customers may include farms, greenhouse operations, growing-media producers, nurseries, garden centres and landscaping companies. Product value rises with processing depth: loose compost is a local low-margin material, while a stabilised, screened, standardised and packaged soil product can reach wider markets.

Logistics can determine viability

Many substrates have low bulk density, high moisture, or both. Long-distance transport of the raw material can therefore erode project economics quickly.

The most robust configuration is usually to locate processing close to the feedstock source or directly at the site where the co-product is generated.

Integrated local clusters can be even more attractive when a food or agricultural processor, a substrate plant, a mushroom or livestock farm, a biogas facility and a fertiliser user are located within a short radius. The same organic stream can then pass through several value-creating stages with limited logistics.

Economic efficiency

The economics of bioconversion differ markedly from those of simple fuel production. Feedstock cost can be low, but process control, hygiene, qualified staff and product sales become more demanding.

Three broad value-creation levels can be distinguished.

1. Direct use of a co-product

Examples include brewers' spent grain → feed and spent mushroom substrate → soil improver. CAPEX is low and so is product value. The main economic benefit may come from avoiding disposal cost and having a nearby user.

2. Processed commercial product

Examples include feedstock → fermented feed and SMS → compost → screening → granulated or packaged fertiliser. More unit operations are required, but the market radius and achievable value can increase.

3. High-technology bioconversion

Examples include co-product → microbial protein, enzymes or specialised bioproducts. Potential product value is much higher, but so are technology risk, process-control requirements, product qualification requirements, CAPEX and the difficulty of market entry.

What determines profitability?

A preliminary project assessment should focus on at least seven factors.

Feedstock cost. The best case is a stable co-product stream with zero or negative acquisition cost, where the producer benefits from reliable off-take.

Feedstock consistency. Biological processes are much more sensitive to compositional variability than a boiler or many thermal-conversion systems.

Transport distance. For wet material, logistics can become one of the largest cost items.

Heat cost. Heat matters especially for pasteurisation and for drying a product or residual stream.

Primary-product yield. Even modest changes in biological productivity can materially affect margin.

Market access. Producing a biological product can be easier than building stable recurring sales for it.

Residual-stream economics. A project in which the spent substrate becomes a product or feedstock for the next process is more robust than one that still pays for disposal.

Investment attractiveness

As a biomass-utilisation route, bioconversion can create higher added value but also higher commercial and technological risk than fuel production.

The strongest projects combine several conditions: a large stable source of homogeneous feedstock; sanitary suitability; low feedstock cost; short logistics; a clearly identified buyer for the primary product; and an economical route for the residual stream.

The most attractive investment may therefore be not a single unit operation but an integrated cascading system.

For example:

straw or another agricultural residue
→ mushroom substrate
→ mushrooms
→ spent substrate
→ biogas
→ energy + organic fertiliser.

Another option is:

food-industry co-product
→ fermentation
→ feed ingredient
→ unconverted residue
→ biogas or fertiliser.

In such systems, energy recovery becomes the final stage of value extraction rather than the first.

Main limitations

Bioconversion is not suitable for every biomass stream. The main constraints are:

  • the need for clean and controlled feedstock;

  • seasonality of some agricultural streams;

  • microbiological contamination risk;

  • the need for stable substrate composition;

  • the difficulty of storing wet feedstock;

  • relatively narrow specialised markets;

  • registration and safety requirements for feed and food products;

  • large quantities of moist residual material.

A project therefore cannot be evaluated only on feedstock availability. It is equally important to know who will buy the primary product and where the spent substrate will go.

Bioconversion within the biomass-utilisation hierarchy

From a resource-efficiency perspective, bioconversion often sits above direct energy use of clean high-quality organic biomass. If a controlled plant co-product is suitable for food, feed or biotechnology and a market exists, direct combustion can destroy potential value.

This does not make bioconversion universally superior. Contaminated, highly variable or remote feedstock may be more rationally directed to fuel production, pyrolysis or other less demanding processes.

A useful value hierarchy is:

food or biotechnology product → feed → materials and fertilisers → biogas → solid fuel and heat.

The exact sequence depends on feedstock composition, market conditions and local economics. The core principle of cascading use is that, before energy recovery, biomass should ideally yield products whose value exceeds the value of the energy contained in it.

In that context, bioconversion becomes not simply a waste-management route but part of a modern circular bioeconomy.
